## Phase 1 doctrine — mic + piano hammer + first six resonances (~3:50–5:52)

Starting point: microphone + small hammer (piano hammer shown) — lightly tap soundboard **around the saddle, over the bridge, and in the lower bout**. Mic in front records acoustic output; analyze peaks — each peak ↔ a main resonance.

Most sound of acoustic and classical guitars produced by the **first six main body resonances**. We know how to tune these for the sound we want:
- More bass → first resonance **lower**
- More expressive mid-range, punchy, projective → more of the **higher** resonance modes (dipoles and tripoles)

Related to design: soundboard, bridge mass/stiffness, bracing design and tuning. Mic shows how resonances amplify string sound — frequencies and how they can be improved.

## Finished vs in-build intervention (~5:52–8:22)

Analysis is for transforming/customizing sound — but on a **finished** guitar don’t be too aggressive: can’t change soundboard thickness or bridge design. What you can do: **tuning** — resonances must not fight notes; resonances should be **in between notes**. When a main body resonance coincides with a fretboard note → fight: lose sustain, intonation not perfect, note doesn’t sound nice (fundamentals and harmonics) — musicians dislike this. Useful finished-guitar tuning.

With mic + **free software** suggested in the book (not expensive system): analysis **during the build**, e.g. **before gluing the bridge**, to check resonance frequency at that stage. Compare to prior instruments at same stage → correct (a bit too much / too low) — e.g. remove a little soundboard material. Wolf-note / general tuning.

## Monopole as sound dictator (~8:23–9:03)

Main resonance produced by the **monopole** — central soundboard around the bridge. Monopole frequency position really dictates guitar sound: lower → more bass; higher → more highs; changes general sound. Can master this during the build (hence book title).

## Wood selection via MB catalog (~9:03–10:33)

Begin at start of build: MB website / MB catalog — know tonewood specs.
- **Fingerstyle:** highly responsive wood, high **sound radiation coefficient (SRC)**, maybe stiff → reduce mass → light responsive soundboard
- **Venue / stage:** less sensitive → more consistent, fewer feedback/Larsen problems → lower SRC
- **Flamenco:** medium to low **damping** → percussive punchy
- **Concert/classical / recording / max tone:** very high **Q**, low damping

Then brace, glue soundboard to edges; powerful tuning stage follows.

## Boxed / sides-in-mold stage (~10:33–11:12)

Mold for sides; when top glued to sides, again mic + small hammer — check if bracing/soundboard on spot or need to move resonance frequency. Powerful, not so difficult; needs a little experience.

## Phase 2 — Chladni for mode shape before tuning (~11:12–14:59)

Chladni visualization: understand how resonances are produced on the soundboard. Monopole almost round around bridge — always center of monopole; not hard to find.

Important mid-range radiators: **dipoles** (cross and long), **tripoles** (cross and long). To tune a mode you need its **shape** — poles change position with bracing style (symmetrical/asymmetrical). Same resonance–note fight can happen with dipole/tripole.

To slightly shift e.g. a **long tripole** down: see vibration shape via speaker + leaves/dust → **nodal lines** (non-vibrating; leaves gather there; bounce off max-displacement zones). Then e.g. add a little **mass on one of the three poles** (one usually enough). Map main resonances to choose best point to tune one mode **without affecting others**. Book has visual support. Need small **light** speaker (hold over soundboard) + small **Bluetooth** amp + smartphone frequency generation. MB kit “really perfect” for this.

## Phase 3 — monopole parameters (mass, stiffness, mobility) (~15:00–19:00)

Third step: analysis of **monopole parameters** — deflection meter + jig measuring deflection at **center of the monopole** by applying a weight and measuring soundboard deflection.

Ordering note: Chladni is visualization of resonances already measured in FRF (peak → shape). Monopole-parameter measurement is about that round bridge-centered resonance — most important radiator (steel and classical).

Parameters:
1. **Frequency** where it resonates  
2. **Mass of the monopole** — not complete soundboard mass; mass of the part vibrating around the bridge. Imagine sawing a circle usually ~**20–25 cm diameter** around the bridge — that is monopole mass  
3. **Stiffness** — resistance to deformation; put weight → deformation → stiffness  

With frequency, mass, stiffness: FRF tells lows/mids/highs balance; **monopole mobility** tells **dynamic character** / responsiveness (high vs low).

Interpretation sketches:
- High mass soundboard → expect less punchy, lots of lows  
- Low monopole mass → high responsiveness, highs, punchy  
- High mobility / very light + hard **strumming** → saturates easily, strange sound if driven hard; great for **fingerpicking** / classical tonal balance across fretboard → look for lower mass  

Jig displacement measurement → complete picture; also to **reproduce** that guitar.

## Philosophy — control dispersion, don’t flatten identity (~19:01–21:42)

Tools mainly to tune shop guitars to their best; reduce wood dispersion while each guitar still tells a different story. Not leveling everything — “180 Hz monopole” ≠ same guitar. CNC / electricity analogies: tools don’t remove magic. Variety in lutherie is fascinating.
