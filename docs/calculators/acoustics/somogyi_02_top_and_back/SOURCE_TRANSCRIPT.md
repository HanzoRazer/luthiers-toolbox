## Energy path + back contribution proof (~0:12–1:54)

Now this is the voice of the soundbox at this point in time. Looked at from a particular point of view: I'm tapping on the top. The path of energies in the soundbox is that the top becomes activated from the strings; this activates the air mass, which activates the back, which then in turn activates the air mass and the top — and the sound energies bounce back and forth. Some of that sound activity is released through the sound port and others are bounced off the exterior surfaces, but there's a lot of bouncing-around activity and the back necessarily is involved in that.

How do we know that? Well, the back now is **damped out** because it's lying on my knees. But if we lift this up and simply allow the back to contribute its voice, we can see that the character of that sound changes quite remarkably — this is a much more **open, loud, sustaining, woody** sound.

So what we're hearing literally is the activity of the top and the back playing **ping-pong** with the energy of my tapping. This is something that all guitars will do whether or not you want them to — unless they're filled with sand or plaster of Paris, but in that case they're not going to be of much interest to anybody except maybe an interior decorator.

## Coupled-oscillator demo (rubber bands + weight) (~1:58–4:34)

One way I have found of illustrating the motions of the top and the back as a function of a driving energy is the following.

This sophisticated and expensive device is a series of rubber bands and an iron weight on the bottom. The principles of physics that this can illustrate are that: if this hand were to be the **top**, this rubber band the **air mass**, and this mass the **back** — and usually the back is a heavier, denser, more massive wood than the face, so this example would be true to that.

The intelligent guitar maker will try to figure out how the top and the back will function **in tandem**, because it's the **relationship of one to the other** that will determine how successful an air pump you've made.

We learned this experiment in high school physics: with an arrangement like this, you could really, really drive the guitar top with a lot of energy and it'll make very little difference to what the back is doing — it's mostly inert. But at the **right frequency relationship**, a rather small amount of drive from this end translates into considerable increase of efficiency in driving this member. This is what the guitar top and the guitar back do with each other.

So it's incumbent on the maker to try to figure out what the **resonance relationship** of those plates ought to be to one another — because if you miss it, then you'll get something that really wastes a lot of the energy. It's tricky; it takes years sometimes to zero in on that; but everybody who's serious about this will wind up going in this direction. People like me fortunately find this actually interesting; it would be deadly boring to a lot of people.

## Method: stiffness target, then brace reduction (~7:14–8:21)

My particular method is to take the top down to a certain **target stiffness**. Most people work to a target **thickness** — that doesn't work, because the same thickness of one material will translate into varying amounts of [stiffness] in another even though they're dimensionally the same. So I want to take all my tops down to the same [stiffness] as a function of a **deflection test**.

And then I will start to brace them. At first the braces are far too massive, and I systematically and slowly reduce them while I listen. It's a very intimate activity — you can't really watch TV while you do that; you have to pay attention to what's going on.
